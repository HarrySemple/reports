    Section 1.2 Model Formulation

    We consider the stationary linear transport equation
    \begin{equation} \mathbf{b} \cdot \nabla u + \sigma_A u = \sigma_T\!\left( \int_{\Omega} \mathcal{K}(x,y)\, u(y)\, \mathrm{d}y - u(x)\right) + q \quad \text{in } \Omega,\tag{1.2.1} \end{equation}
    subject to inflow boundary condition
    \begin{equation} u = g \quad \text{on } \Gamma_- := \{x \in \partial\Omega : \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{n} \lt 0\}.\tag{1.2.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Here \(\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) is a bounded Lipschitz domain with outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\text{.}\) The data are as follows.
    1. \(\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) is a constant wind field.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\sigma_A \in L^\infty(\Omega)\text{,}\) \(\sigma_A \geq 0\text{,}\) is the absorption cross section.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\sigma_T \in L^\infty(\Omega)\text{,}\) \(\sigma_T \geq 0\text{,}\) is the scattering cross section.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\mathcal{K} \in L^\infty(\Omega \times \Omega)\text{,}\) \(\mathcal{K} \geq 0\text{,}\) is the scattering kernel, normalised so that
      \begin{equation} \int_\Omega \mathcal{K}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y = 1 \qquad \text{for all} x \in \Omega.\tag{1.2.3} \end{equation}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(q \in L^2(\Omega)\) is an interior source and \(g \in L^2(\Gamma_-)\) is the inflow datum.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The combined absorption and scattering cross sections form the total (extinction) cross section~\cite{ROUSSIN2003581}
    \begin{equation} \sigma(x) := \sigma_A(x) + \sigma_T(x),\tag{1.2.4} \end{equation}
    which appears throughout the analysis. We assume the positivity condition
    \begin{equation} \label{eqn:positivity} \sigma(x) \;\geq\; \mu \;\gt\; 0 \qquad \forall x\in \Omega.\tag{1.2.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Equation (1.2.1) generalises classical diffusion by replacing the local Fick’s-law closure with an explicit nonlocal scattering term. Rather than approximating the effect of collisions by a gradient of \(u\text{,}\) the integral \(\int_\Omega \mathcal{K}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\) accounts for particles arriving at \(x\) from a neighbourhood weighted by the probability density \(\mathcal{K}(x,\cdot)\text{.}\) The subtracted term \(-u(x)\) accounts for particles lost from \(x\) due to scattering, so that the net scattering contribution vanishes when \(u\) is spatially uniform and \(K\) is normalised as in (1.2.3).
    🔗
    This nonlocal structure is physically significant under three modelling regimes pertinent to shielding applications, which are in direct contradiction of the diffusion approximation~\cite{dematte2026diffusion}. One instance is where absorption is not negligible relative to scattering, meaning the diffusion approximation \(\sigma_A \ll \sigma_T\) breaks down. Second, where the mean free path is comparable to the domain length scale, so that particles travel non-negligible distances between collisions. Third, where microstructure induces long-range spatial correlations in the collision probability, as in heterogeneous or stochastic media.
    🔗
    The choice of kernel \(\mathcal{K}\) determines the character of the nonlocal interaction. Gaussian kernels model short-range scattering with an exponentially decaying influence. Algebraically decaying kernels \(\mathcal{K}(x,y) \propto |x-y|^{-(2+2s)}\text{,}\) arise from the fractional Laplacian and capture superdiffusive or long-tailed transport. A final trivial choice in the case of \(\Omega=[0,1]^2\) is the constant unit kernel \(\mathcal{K} = 1\text{,}\) deflating the scattering operator to simply integrating \(u\text{.}\) Although this operation is still nonlocal, it makes for a straightforward test case in validating our analytical results.
