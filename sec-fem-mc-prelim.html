    Section 1.3 Preliminaries

    Subsection 1.3.1 Finite Element Methods

    The finite element method (FEM) seeks an approximate weak solution \(u_h\) to a PDE by selecting from finite-dimensional subspace \(V_h \subset V\) of a suitable Hilbert space \(V\)\cite{pryer2022fem}. For the model Poisson problem \(-\Delta u = f\) on \(\Omega\) with \(u|_{\partial\Omega} = 0\text{,}\) this gives: find \(u \in H^1_0(\Omega)\) such that
    \begin{equation} a(u,v) := \int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_\Omega f v\,\mathrm{d}x =: l(v) \qquad \forall\, v \in H^1_0(\Omega).\tag{1.3.1} \end{equation}
    The Lax-Milgram theorem guarantees a unique solution whenever \(a(\cdot,\cdot)\) is coercive and continuous on \(V\text{,}\) and \(l\) is a bounded linear functional. Restricting to a conforming finite element space \(V_h \subset H^1_0(\Omega)\) and choosing a basis \(\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^{N_h}\) yields the linear system \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}\) with \(A_{ij} = a(\phi_j,\phi_i)\text{.}\) The coercivity and continuity of \(a(\cdot,\cdot)\) results in an \(\mathbf{A}\) positive-definite and bounded. The C\’{e}a lemma provides the best-approximation estimate
    \begin{equation} \| u - u_h \|_V \;\leq\; \frac{\gamma}{\alpha} \inf_{w_h \in V_h} \| u - w_h \|_V,\tag{1.3.2} \end{equation}
    where \(\alpha\) and \(\gamma\) are the coercivity and continuity constants of \(a(\cdot,\cdot)\) respectively.

    Subsection 1.3.2 SUPG Stabilisation

    For advection-dominated problems, the standard Galerkin formulation becomes unstable. The bilinear form associated with the operator \(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla u + \sigma u\) is not necessarily coercive in \(H^1(\Omega)\text{,}\) and the Galerkin solution may exhibit spurious oscillations~\cite{ern2004theory}. The Streamline Upwind Petrov-Galerkin (SUPG) method~\cite{brooks1982streamline, johnson1983finite} addresses this by modifying the test function to \(v + \delta_K \mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla v\) on each element \(K\text{,}\) where \(\delta_K > 0\) is a mesh-dependent stabilisation parameter.
    🔗
    The effect of SUPG is to add an artificial diffusion of strength \(\delta_K\) along streamlines of the solution. Consistency is preserved - as the additional term vanishes when evaluating the exact solution - while coercivity is ensured for a sufficient strength \(\delta_K\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.3 Monte Carlo Integration

    Monte Carlo integration estimates an integral \(I = \int_\Omega f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\) by the sample mean
    \begin{equation} I_M := \frac{|\Omega|}{M}\sum_{j=1}^M f(Y_j), \qquad Y_j \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Unif}(\Omega).\tag{1.3.3} \end{equation}
    The estimator is unbiased with \(\mathbb{E}[I_M] = I\text{,}\) and its standard deviation satisfies
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}\bigl[|I - I_M|^2\bigr]^{1/2} = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(f)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{M}},\tag{1.3.4} \end{equation}
    where \(\mathrm{Var}(f) = \int_\Omega (f - I/|\Omega|)^2\, \mathrm{d}y\)~\cite{gobet2016monte}. Notably, this \(\mathcal{O}(M^{-1/2})\) rate is independent of dimension, making Monte Carlo the method of choice for high-dimensional integration.

    Subsection 1.3.4 Useful Identities

    Finally, the following identities are used repeatedly in the analysis and stated here for convenience. The first is the Green’s formula for the advection operator. Given \(v, w \in H^1(\Omega)\) and \(\mathbf{b} \in [W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)]^2\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation} \int_{\Omega} (\mathbf{b} \cdot \nabla v)\, w \, \mathrm{d}x = \int_{\partial \Omega} v w (\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{n})\,\mathrm{d}S - \int_{\Omega} (\mathbf{b} \cdot \nabla w)\, v\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_{\Omega} v w \, \nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}\,\mathrm{d}x .\tag{1.3.5} \end{equation}
    For much of our analysis, \(\mathbf{b}\) is taken to be a constant advection direction, thus the final term vanishes.
    🔗
    In addition, Young’s inequality states that, for any \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\) we have
    \begin{equation} |ab| \leq \frac{a^2}{2} + \frac{b^2}{2}.\tag{1.3.6} \end{equation}
