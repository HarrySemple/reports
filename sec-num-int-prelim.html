    Section 2.2 Preliminaries

    Subsection 2.2.1 Introduction

    The aim of numerical integration is to approximate the integral
    \begin{equation} I = \int_\Omega f(x) \ \mathrm{d}x\tag{2.2.1} \end{equation}
    where \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^d\) (predominantly \(d=1,2\)) and \(f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{.}\) We focus on quadrature rules, which approximate the integrand by a polynomial \(p(x) \in \mathbb{P}^d\) and integrate \(p\) exactly:
    \begin{equation} Qp = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i p(x_i) = \int_\Omega p(x) \ \mathrm{d}x \approx I.\tag{2.2.2} \end{equation}
    A quadrature rule has an associated degree of precision (DoP) \(d\text{,}\) meaning all polynomials of degree \(\leq d\) are integrated exactly. In this section, we provide a background on constructing quadrature rules to a specified DoP. We begin with the well-understood 1D case before turning to the more involved problem of quadrature in 2D.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.2 Quadrature in 1D

    Subsubsection 2.2.2.1 Introduction

    In the following, we take \(\Omega = [-1,1]\text{,}\) so \(I = \int_{-1}^1 f(x) \mathrm{d}x\text{.}\) For a quadrature rule to have DoP \(d\text{,}\) we require \(Qp = Ip\) for all \(p\in\mathbb{P}^d\text{.}\) By linearity of integration, it suffices to check this over a basis of \(\mathbb{P}^d\text{.}\) A natural choice is the monomial basis \(\{1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^d\}\text{,}\) resulting in the \textit{momentum equations} \cite{engels1980numerical} (also referred to as moment matching equations \cite{tchernychova2016caratheodory})
    \begin{equation} \label{eqn:quad-sys-1} Qx^k = \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n x_n^k = c_k, \qquad \forall k \in \{0, \ldots, d\},\tag{2.2.3} \end{equation}
    where \(x_1, \ldots, x_N\) are distinct evaluation points, \(w_1, \ldots, w_N\) are their associated weights and
    \begin{equation} \label{eqn:cases} c_k = Ix^k = \begin{cases} \frac{2}{k+1} \quad &k \text{ even}\\ 0 \quad &k \text{ odd}. \end{cases}\tag{2.2.4} \end{equation}
    Writing (2.2.3) explicitly, we have
    \begin{equation} \label{eqn:quad-sys-2} \begin{aligned} w_1 + w_2 + \cdots + w_N &= c_0, \\ w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_N x_N &= c_1, \\ w_1 x_1^2 + w_2 x_2^2 + \cdots + w_N x_N^2 &= c_2, \\ &\vdots \\ w_1 x_1^{d} + w_2 x_2^{d} + \cdots + w_N x_N^{d} &= c_d . \end{aligned}\tag{2.2.5} \end{equation}
    The task of constructing a quadrature rule with DoP \(d\) is to solve this system for \(N\) evaluation points and weights.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.2.2.2 Fixed Nodes

    We first consider fixed evaluation points. Setting \(N=d+1\) results in an exactly-determined linear system in the weights \(w_1, \ldots, w_N\text{,}\) as in (2.2.3). This results in a quadrature rule of DoP \(d\) with \(N=d+1\) nodes.
    🔗
    A special case arises through considering an odd number of symmetric evaluation points. Suppose we have \(2N+1\) points \(x_{-N}, \ldots, x_0, \ldots, x_N\text{,}\) with \(x_0 = 0\) and \(x_{-n} = -x_{n}\) for all \(n\in\{1, \ldots, N\}\text{.}\) The system becomes
    \begin{equation} \label{eqn:special-fixed-quad} Qx^k = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} w_n x_n^k = c_k \qquad \forall k \in \{0, \ldots, 2N\}.\tag{2.2.6} \end{equation}
    By further imposing symmetric weights \(w_{-n} = w_n\text{,}\) the odd-power equations are automatically satisfied, since \(w_{-n}x_{-n}^{2k+1} = -w_{n}x_{n}^{2k+1}\) and \(w_0x_0^{2k+1} = 0\text{,}\) consistent with (2.2.4). The even-power equations reduce to
    \begin{equation} \label{eqn:special-fixed-quad-2} Qx^{2k} = w_0x_0^{2k} + \sum_{n=1}^{N} 2w_n x_n^{2k} = c_{2k} \qquad \forall k \in \{0, \ldots, N\},\tag{2.2.7} \end{equation}
    where the \(N+1\) unknowns \(w_0, \ldots, w_N\) can be determined exactly from \(N+1\) linear equations. Thus, the derived weights \(w_{-N}, \ldots, w_N\) solve (2.2.6) with DoP \(2N\text{.}\) Moreover, as all odd-power integrands zero out, the rule in fact achieves DoP \(2N+1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus, for \(2N+1\) evaluation points, enforcing weight symmetry achieves DoP \(2N+1\text{.}\) In the case of equidistant points, this derives Newton-Cotes quadrature rule, while for Chebyshev points, this gives us the Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature rule \cite{clenshaw1960method}.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.2.2.3 Free Nodes

    We return to the momentum equations (2.2.5) and consider the case where both nodes and weights are free parameters. We show that the nodes can be chosen as roots of a suitable polynomial, which underpins the construction of Gaussian quadrature rules~\cite{engels1980numerical}.
    🔗
    We want to find \(2N\) unknowns: nodes \(x_1,\ldots,x_N\) and weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_N\text{.}\) Let \(q(x)\) be the unique monic polynomial of degree \(N\) with roots \(x_1, \ldots, x_N\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation} q(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \cdots + \beta_{N-1} x^{N-1} + x^N .\tag{2.2.8} \end{equation}
    Multiplying the \(k\)th momentum equation in (2.2.5) by \(\beta_k\) for each \(k\in\{0,\ldots,N\}\) gives us
    \begin{equation} \beta_k w_1 x_1^k + \cdots + \beta_k w_N x_N^k = \beta_k c_k, \qquad k\in\{0,\ldots,N\}. \tag{2.2.9} \end{equation}
    Adding these equations together for all \(k\in\{0,\ldots,N\}\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} & w_1 \bigl( \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_{N-1}x_1^{N-1} + x_1^N \bigr) \\ &\quad + w_2 \bigl( \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_2 + \cdots + x_2^N \bigr) \\ &\quad \vdots \\ &\quad + w_N \bigl( \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_N + \cdots + x_N^N \bigr) = \beta_0 c_0 + \cdots + \beta_{N-1} c_{N-1} + c_N . \end{aligned}\tag{2.2.10} \end{equation}
    Since \(q(x_i)=0\) for all \(i\text{,}\) the left-hand side vanishes, arriving at
    \begin{equation} 0 = \beta_0 c_0 + \cdots + \beta_{N-1} c_{N-1} + c_N .\tag{2.2.11} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Repeating this argument for shifts \(j = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1\) - multiplying the \(k+j\)th momentum equation by \(\beta_k\) and summing - we arrive at the linear system
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \beta_0 c_0 + \cdots + \beta_{N-1} c_{N-1} + c_N &= 0, \\ \beta_0 c_1 + \cdots + \beta_{N-1} c_N + c_{N+1} &= 0, \\ &\vdots \\ \beta_0 c_{N-1} + \cdots + \beta_{N-1} c_{2N-2} + c_{2N-1} &= 0 , \end{aligned}\tag{2.2.12} \end{equation}
    or in matrix form,
    \begin{equation} \begin{pmatrix} c_0 & \cdots & c_{N-1} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ c_{N-1} & \cdots & c_{2N-2} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_{N-1} \end{pmatrix} = - \begin{pmatrix} c_N \\ \vdots \\ c_{2N-1} \end{pmatrix}.\tag{2.2.13} \end{equation}
    This determines the unique set of coefficients \(\beta_0, \ldots, \beta_{N-1}\text{,}\) from which the roots \(x_1, \ldots, x_N\) of \(q(x)\) are found. With the nodes determined, the weights follow from previous argument for (2.2.5) with fixed nodes.
    🔗
    The above process constructs Gauss-Legendre quadrature, with nodes \(x_1, \ldots, x_N\) found to be roots of \(N^{\text{th}}\) degree Legendre polynomial~\cite{engels1980numerical}. More generally, introducing a weight function \(\omega(x)\) into the integrand leads to other Gaussian quadrature rules, with nodes given by roots of the corresponding polynomials. This includes Gauss-Chebyshev (Chebyshev polynomials), Gauss-Jacobi, Gauss-Laguerre, and Gauss-Hermite quadrature~\cite{engels1980numerical}.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.3 Cubature in 2D

    We now consider cubature rules for domains \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\) Constructing efficient cubature rules in 2D is significantly more challenging than in 1D and remains an active area of research~\cite{bauman2020compressed}. We demonstrate this with the example of the "plus" domain, composed of unit squares (Figure 2.2.1).
    🔗
    A natural starting point is geometric construction~\cite{engels1980numerical}. In Figure 2.2.1 the plus domain is tessellated with 10 identical triangles, and a quadrature rule is constructed by assigning each vertex a weight proportional to the total area of incident triangles. Vertices labelled \(c\) are incident to one triangle, \(b_1\) to two, \(b_2\) to 4 and \(b_3\) to five, giving
    \begin{equation} Qf = \frac{|\Omega|}{10} \cdot \frac{1}{3} \cdot \left(\sum_{\mathbf{x_c}\in c} f(\mathbf{x_c}) + 2\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{b_1}\in b_1} f(\mathbf{x}_{b_1}) + 4\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{b_2}\in b_2} f(\mathbf{x}_{b_2}) + 5\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{b_3}\in b_3} f(\mathbf{x}_{b_3})\right).\tag{2.2.14} \end{equation}
    Here, \(|\Omega|/10\) is the area of each triangle, and each triangle contributes the average of \(f\) at its three vertices. This rule has DoP 1 by construction, integrating linear functions exactly, but not quadratics.
    🔗
    This is a far cry from the efficiency of quadrature rules in 1D. In Gaussian quadrature, \(N\) nodes yield DoP \(2N-1\text{,}\) whereas here 12 nodes achieve only DoP 1. Treating these as fixed nodes, 12 weights can satisfy 12 momentum equations. Since DoP \(d\) requires \((d+1)(d+2)/2\) momentum equations, this corresponds to at most DoP 3.
    🔗
    However, the difficulty of constructing efficient cubature rules in 2D is due to the potential complexity of the domain. Unlike a line segment, momentum integrals \(c_{\alpha\beta} = \int_\Omega x^\alpha y^\beta \mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\) over a general \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2\) may be difficult or impossible to evaluate analytically, making it impractical to assemble the momentum equations (2.2.5) needed to find suitable nodes and weights.
    🔗
    For rectangular domains, higher DoP can be achieved by extending 1D quadrature rules along each axis via Cartesian products~\cite{tchernychova2016caratheodory} (also called tensor products~\cite{trefethen2017cubature}). For a Gaussian quadrature rule \(Q\) with DoP \(d \ge \max\{k_1, k_2\}\text{,}\) we have that
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \int_a^b\int_u^v x^{k_1}y^{k_2} \mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y &= \int_a^b x^{k_1} \mathrm{d}x \int_u^v y^{k_2} \mathrm{d}y \\ &= Qx^{k_1} Qy^{k_2} \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N} w_n w_m x_n^{k_1} y_m^{k_2} =: \tilde{Q}x^{k_1}y^{k_2}. \end{aligned}\tag{2.2.15} \end{equation}
    This yields a cubature rule \(\tilde{Q}\) with DoP \(2N-1\) on the rectangular domain \([a,b]\times[u,v]\) using \(N^2\) nodes. We will refer to this as Gaussian cubature.
    🔗
    One option for complex domains is to tessellate with rectangles and apply Gaussian cubature to each, giving a patchwork of cubature rules, as illustrated in Figure 2.2.2. Although it has been shown that Gaussian cubature rules can be constructed directly for any polygonal domain~\cite{sommariva2007product}, tessellation remains a practical approach for non-polygonal, irregular or disconnected domains.
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.3. Higher Dimensions.

    We briefly discuss cubature in higher dimensions. For rectangular domains, a Cartesian-product Gauss-Legendre rule achieves DoP \(d\) in \(s\) dimensions using \(\ceil{(d+1)/2}^s\) nodes. However, this count grows exponentially with \(s\text{,}\) making it impractical for high dimensions and motivating the use of recombination to compress such rules, as discussed in Section~3.4.
    🔗
    Alternative approaches include sparse grids and Monte Carlo methods~\cite{trefethen2017cubature}. Sparse grids use a hierarchical construction to reduce the node count, but the exponential scaling persists in the worst case. Monte Carlo methods achieve a convergence rate independent of dimension, at the cost of stochastic noise and potentially many samples for high accuracy.
